Why do we control weeds?

Weeds are not just "bad plants." They are disturbance specialists that outcompete native vegetation, reduce agricultural productivity, create fire hazards, and degrade ecosystems. Effective control is about strategic intervention, not eradication for its own sake.

Economic impact

Weeds cost Australian agriculture billions annually in lost production and control expenses. In Tasmania, gorse, blackberry, and serrated tussock are among the most economically damaging species.

Example: A dense gorse infestation can reduce pasture carrying capacity by 50-70%, directly impacting farm profitability. Control costs for gorse across a single property can exceed $50,000 over a 10-year program.

Beyond pasture: Weeds also impact forestry, tourism, and infrastructure maintenance (roadsides, fencelines, waterways).

Environmental impact

Invasive weeds displace native vegetation, alter fire regimes, reduce biodiversity, and can change entire ecosystem functions. They are a major threat to Tasmania's unique natural heritage.

Example: Boneseed and bridal creeper smother native understorey plants, reducing habitat for native fauna. Willow infestations choke Tasmanian rivers, altering stream hydrology and displacing native riparian species.

Ecosystem transformation: Some weeds don't just compete — they change soil chemistry, hydrology, and fire frequency, creating conditions that favour themselves and disadvantage natives.

Fire hazard

Many weeds (gorse, broom, blackberry) create dense, highly flammable biomass that increases fire intensity and frequency. In Tasmania's dry summers, this is a critical safety issue.

Key concern: Gorse infested areas burn hotter and faster than native vegetation, threatening infrastructure and lives. Post-fire, gorse often regenerates more densely than before, creating a worsening cycle.

Fire alone does not control gorse — it stimulates germination and resprouting. Fire must be followed by herbicide treatment within 6-12 months.
Water and soil degradation

Weeds like willows alter stream hydrology and bank stability. Blackberry and gorse can impede water flow and increase erosion. Their control is essential for catchment health.

Waterway focus: Willows choke Tasmanian rivers, causing flooding and displacing native riparian vegetation. Their dense root mats can destabilise banks and alter channel morphology.

Soil impacts: Some weeds (e.g., capeweed) can alter soil microbial communities and nutrient cycling, creating feedback loops that favour their persistence.

Weed ecology: Why weeds win (and how to stop them)

Understanding why weeds are successful is essential for effective control. Weeds are not "stronger" than native plants — they are opportunists that exploit disturbance.

Disturbance creates opportunity

Weeds germinate first after disturbance (fire, grading, flood, overgrazing). They grow fast, reproduce quickly, and tolerate harsh conditions. Native species often need stable conditions and time to establish.

Implication: Any activity that creates bare ground is a weed recruitment event. Plan for it.

The seedbank is the real enemy

Visible weeds are only the tip of the iceberg. A single gorse bush can produce 5,000-10,000 seeds annually, and seeds remain viable in Tasmanian soils for 30-40+ years.

Implication: Removing adult plants without follow-up guarantees reinfestation. Plan for 5-10 year programs.

Competition prevents invasion

The best defense against weeds is healthy, competitive vegetation. When native or pasture cover is dense, weed seedlings struggle to establish.

Implication: Revegetation and maintaining ground cover are forms of weed control. Cultural methods matter.

Timing is everything

Control must occur before seed set. Once seeds are produced, you've lost that season's battle. Systemic herbicides also require actively growing plants — spraying dormant or stressed weeds wastes chemical.

Implication: Monitor plant growth stages. Control when the plant is vulnerable, not when it's convenient.

Weeds in the Tasmanian context

Tasmania's unique challenges
  • Island biogeography: Native species evolved without many mainland competitors, making them vulnerable to invaders.
  • Cool temperate climate: Many weeds thrive in Tasmania's mild, moist conditions with long growing seasons.
  • Agricultural landscape: Extensive pasture and cropping areas create disturbance that weeds exploit.
  • World Heritage Areas: Pristine environments require minimal-impact control methods. Chemical use near waterways and in reserves is tightly regulated.
  • Fire regimes: The interaction between weeds and bushfire is a critical management concern. Gorse and broom increase fire risk and post-fire regeneration.
  • Variable climate: Tasmania's rainfall gradient (from >3000mm on the West Coast to <500mm in the Midlands) means weed management strategies must be site-specific.
Legal and social context

Tasmania has strong legislation governing weed control:

  • Biosecurity Act 2019 (Tas) — establishes General Biosecurity Duty and controls declared pests. Landholders must control declared weeds on their land.
  • Biosecurity Act 2019 (Tas) — Statutory Weed Management Plans (SWMPs) for species like Gorse and Blackberry remain in force under this Act. These plans set out whether your municipality is Zone A (eradication) or Zone B (containment) for each declared weed.
  • Land managers have a legal obligation to control declared weeds. Failure can result in infringement notices and fines.
  • Community expectations — good weed management is part of being a responsible land manager. Neighbour disputes over weed spread are common.
Note: Non-declared environmental weeds still require management under general land management responsibilities, even if not legally mandated. Funding bodies and certification schemes (e.g., Landcare, NRM grants) often require weed control.
Key takeaway: Weed control is not about eliminating all weeds everywhere. It's about strategic management to protect economic, environmental, and social values. The goal is "tolerable levels" defined by your context and obligations. For declared weeds, the threshold is usually zero (eradication). For environmental weeds in sensitive areas, it may be containment. For agricultural weeds in pasture, it may be suppression below an economic threshold.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

IPM is not a method. It's a decision-making framework. It's the strategic approach that underpins all effective weed control. Understanding IPM is fundamental to this unit.

What is IPM?

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is an approach to strategically control pests by combining multiple methods, monitoring populations, and using chemicals as a last resort. It minimises environmental harm while achieving control objectives.

Core principle: IPM is not "no chemicals" — it's "the right intervention at the right time, using the least harmful effective method."

Key components of IPM

1. Understand the pest

Know your weed: its lifecycle, growth habits, seedbank persistence, and preferred conditions. You cannot control what you don't understand.

Practical application: Before treating, confirm species identification. Know whether it's a perennial (resprouts from roots) or annual (relies on seed). Know its flowering and seeding times so you can target the vulnerable stage.

2. Prevent infestation

Hygiene, quarantine, early detection, and maintaining healthy vegetation are more cost-effective than control. Prevention is the foundation of IPM.

Practical application: Clean machinery and footwear between sites. Source clean gravel, soil, and mulch. Don't move seed-bearing material. Maintain ground cover. Monitor fencelines and entry points for new infestations.

3. Set thresholds

Determine tolerable levels. Not every weed requires immediate action. Control only when the infestation exceeds a defined threshold.

Practical application: For declared weeds, the threshold is zero — you must control them. For pasture weeds, the threshold may be when infestation reduces carrying capacity below an economic level. For environmental weeds, it may be preventing spread into high-value areas.

4. Monitor extensively

Regular inspection is essential. You must know what's there, how much, and whether your control is working. Monitoring informs every decision.

Practical application: Establish photo points. Use quadrats for density estimates. Record observations (growth stage, weather, treatment applied). Monitor immediately after treatment (application check), at 3 months (kill assessment), and at 12 months (regrowth).

5. Use a range of methods

Combine cultural, mechanical, biological, and chemical controls. No single method works for all situations. Diversity reduces resistance and improves outcomes.

Practical application: Use grazing or slashing to reduce biomass, followed by herbicide on regrowth. Use manual removal for isolated plants and herbicide for dense infestations. Rotate herbicide modes of action.

6. Chemicals as last resort

Herbicides are valuable tools, but should be used strategically and minimally. Always consider non-chemical options first, and use chemicals only when justified.

Practical application: In sensitive areas (near water, native vegetation), use non-chemical methods. If chemical is necessary, use the least toxic effective product, spot-spray rather than broadcast, and respect buffer zones.

The IPM decision cycle

IPM is a continuous cycle, not a one-off action:

IPM in practice: The best weed control programs are long-term, multi-method, and monitoring-driven. They accept that success is measured over years, not weeks. They plan for follow-up before starting initial treatment.

Control methods: A toolkit

No single method works everywhere. IPM requires selecting the right tool for the situation. Here are the four main categories, with detailed information on advantages, limitations, and Tasmanian-specific considerations.

Biological control

What it is: Using natural enemies (insects, mites, diseases) to control weeds.

Examples: Gorse seed weevil, gorse spider mite, gorse thrips. Blackberry rust fungus has been released but has been largely ineffective across its range.

Advantages: Self-sustaining, no chemicals, can be integrated with other methods. Useful for large, inaccessible areas where other methods are impractical.

Limitations: Slow-acting (often takes years to see impact), host-specific, requires approval under the Biological Control Act 1984 (Cth). Rarely achieves eradication — best used as a suppressive tool within an integrated program.

Tasmanian context: Before using chemicals, check if biological control agents are present. Contact NRE Tas or the Tasmanian Institute of Agriculture (TIA) biological control program for advice.
Cultural control

What it is: Modifying the environment to make it less hospitable to weeds.

Examples: Crop rotation, cover cropping, competitive planting, grazing management, mulching, maintaining ground cover.

Advantages: Addresses the root cause (disturbance and lack of competition), low environmental impact, sustainable. Builds long-term resilience.

Limitations: Requires planning and time, may not be suitable for all sites (e.g., sensitive native vegetation areas). Results are not immediate.

Key principle: The best defense against weeds is healthy, competitive vegetation. Cultural control builds this. In pasture, maintaining strong grass cover outcompetes many weeds. In native areas, revegetation with local species closes the gap that weeds exploit.
Mechanical control

What it is: Physically removing or destroying weeds.

Examples: Hand weeding, mowing, slashing, digging, hoeing, machinery (bobcat, excavator, mulcher).

Advantages: No chemicals, immediate visible results, suitable for sensitive areas (near water, native vegetation). Can be targeted precisely.

Limitations: Labour-intensive, can cause soil disturbance (triggering more weeds), not suitable for all species (e.g., plants that resprout from root fragments). Slashing after seed set spreads weeds.

Critical: Slashing after seed set spreads weeds. Mechanical control must be timed correctly. Mowing and slashing are not suitable for plants that regrow from roots or fragments (e.g., poplars, willows) — and can make them harder to control.
Chemical control

What it is: Using herbicides to kill weeds.

Examples: Glyphosate (broad-spectrum), triclopyr (woody weeds), metsulfuron-methyl (blackberry), flupropanate (serrated tussock).

Advantages: Effective, scalable, can be targeted (spot spray, cut stump, basal bark). Often the most reliable method for established infestations.

Limitations: Off-target damage, environmental risks (water contamination), resistance development, legal requirements (label, buffer zones, permits). Requires training and PPE.

Always: Follow label instructions, use appropriate PPE, respect buffer zones, and consider non-chemical alternatives first. Only use herbicides registered for the specific weed and situation. Off-label use is not permitted in Tasmania for different application methods.

Herbicide resistance: A growing concern

Herbicide resistance is not a future problem — it is happening now in Australia. Glyphosate-resistant annual ryegrass is widespread in cropping systems, and resistance is emerging in other weeds.

How resistance develops: Repeated use of the same herbicide (or same mode of action) selects for individuals that survive. These survivors reproduce, and the resistant population grows. Sub-lethal doses (under-dosing) accelerate this process.

How to manage resistance:

  • Rotate modes of action (MOA) — Don't use the same herbicide group repeatedly. Alternate between Group 1 (e.g., fluazifop), Group 4 (e.g., 2,4-D), Group 9 (glyphosate), etc.
  • Use label rates — Never under-dose. Sub-lethal rates kill susceptible plants but allow resistant ones to survive and reproduce.
  • Combine methods — Herbicides plus mechanical, cultural, or biological controls reduce selection pressure.
  • Monitor for survivors — Patchy kill with a consistent pattern (same equipment, same mix, same day) is a warning sign. Report suspected resistance to NRE Tas.
Tasmanian context: Glyphosate-resistant annual ryegrass has been confirmed in Tasmanian cropping systems. For woody weeds like gorse and broom, resistance is not yet widespread but the risk is real if the same herbicides are used repeatedly.

Planning a weed control program

Effective weed control is planned, not improvised. This section covers the planning process from site assessment to follow-up scheduling.

1. Assess the site
  • Identify the weed — Correct identification is essential. Use NRE Tas resources, field guides, or expert advice. Misidentification wastes time and money.
  • Determine scope and size — Map the infestation. How large? How dense? What stage of growth? Are there satellite populations?
  • Identify non-target species — What native plants and animals are present? Are there threatened species? Is the site near a waterway?
  • Assess site sensitivity — Proximity to waterways, steep slopes, cultural heritage sites, conservation areas.
  • Determine tolerable levels — What level of infestation is acceptable? (Zero for declared weeds; higher for environmental weeds in some contexts; economic thresholds for pasture weeds.)
2. Risk assessment

Every control program needs a risk assessment. Consider:

  • Human safety — Chemical exposure, manual handling, terrain hazards, remote work, first aid availability
  • Environmental risks — Off-target damage, water contamination, soil disturbance, erosion
  • Non-target species — Native plants, animals, beneficial insects
  • Legal compliance — Buffer zones, permits, record-keeping requirements, declared weed obligations
The risk assessment should be documented and reviewed before any control work begins. It should identify controls for each hazard and assign responsibility.
3. Select methods and timing

Based on your assessment, choose methods that:

  • Are effective for the target species
  • Minimise risk to non-target species
  • Comply with legal requirements
  • Are feasible with available resources

Timing is critical: Control before flowering and seeding, when plants are actively growing. For systemic herbicides, this maximizes translocation. For mechanical control, timing avoids seed spread.

Tasmanian seasonal considerations: Autumn (March-May) and spring (September-November) are generally optimal for systemic herbicides. Winter spraying is often ineffective due to dormancy. Summer spraying risks heat stress and drought-stressed plants.

4. Plan follow-up

No follow-up plan = guaranteed failure. Weeds will regrow from seedbanks and root fragments.

  • Schedule monitoring at 3, 6, and 12 months
  • Plan for follow-up treatment (spot spray, hand pull, etc.)
  • Budget for multi-year programs (especially for seedbank-persistent species)
  • Document everything — records are essential for tracking progress and meeting legal obligations
Gorse seedbank can persist for 30+ years in Tasmanian soils. Plan for long-term management, not one-off treatment. Programs that stop at Year 2 fail. Real success is Year 7-10.

Field craft: Equipment, calibration, and application

Spray equipment calibration

Calibration ensures you apply the correct rate of herbicide. An uncalibrated sprayer can under-dose (leading to resistance) or over-dose (wasting chemical and risking off-target damage).

Basic calibration steps:

  1. Measure a known area (e.g., 100m²) or distance (e.g., 50m).
  2. Fill the sprayer with water to a known level.
  3. Spray the area at your normal walking pace and nozzle setting.
  4. Measure how much water was used.
  5. Calculate the application rate (L/ha or L/100m²).
  6. Adjust nozzle size, pressure, or walking speed to achieve the target rate.
Pro tip: Check nozzles regularly for wear and blockages. A worn nozzle can deliver 20% more or less than intended, affecting efficacy and resistance selection.
Spray drift management

Spray drift is the movement of herbicide droplets off-target. It can damage sensitive crops, native vegetation, and waterways.

Key controls:

  • Wind speed: Avoid spraying when wind is >15 km/h (or as specified on the label). Also avoid very still conditions (temperature inversions) when droplets can hang in the air and drift.
  • Nozzle selection: Use low-drift nozzles (air induction, pre-orifice) for coarse droplets.
  • Boom height: Keep the boom as low as practical while maintaining coverage.
  • Buffer zones: Respect label buffer zones near waterways, sensitive areas, and neighbouring properties.
  • Weather conditions: Avoid spraying when temperatures are high and humidity is low (droplets evaporate before landing).
Clean-down and hygiene

Weeds spread via contaminated machinery, footwear, and equipment. Clean-down is a legal requirement under the Biosecurity Act (General Biosecurity Duty) and a practical necessity.

Best practice:

  • Clean machinery before leaving an infested site and before entering a clean site.
  • Remove soil and plant material from tyres, tracks, undercarriage, and buckets.
  • Use a designated wash-down area (not near waterways).
  • Clean footwear and clothing before leaving the site.
  • Record clean-down activities in a logbook.
Tasmanian context: NRE Tas has wash-down guidelines for weed and disease control. These are particularly important for contractors working across multiple properties.
Record keeping

Records are essential for:

  • Legal compliance — Commercial operators must keep records of herbicide use for at least 3 years (product, rate, date, location, weather conditions).
  • Monitoring — Records allow you to track what worked and what didn't.
  • Continuity — If someone else takes over the program, they need to know what's been done.
  • Reporting — Funding bodies and land managers often require progress reports.

What to record: Date, time, location (GPS or map reference), target species, herbicide product and rate, adjuvant used, water volume, area treated, weather conditions (temperature, wind, cloud cover), operator name, equipment used, any issues observed.

Tasmanian and Commonwealth legislation

Weed management is governed by legislation. Understanding your legal obligations is essential — both to avoid penalties and to protect the environment.

Key legislation
LegislationRelevance to weed management
Biosecurity Act 2019 (Tas)The principal legislation for weed management in Tasmania. It establishes the General Biosecurity Duty (GBD) and continues the legal force of Statutory Weed Management Plans (SWMPs) for declared species. Under this Act, landholders must comply with the specific management requirements (Zone A = eradication, Zone B = containment) outlined in the SWMP for their municipality. It is an offence to spread declared weeds.
Agricultural and Veterinary Chemicals (Control of Use) Act 1995 (Tas)Regulates herbicide use. Requires following label instructions and protecting non-target areas. Authorizes NRE Tas to identify noxious weeds and establish Weed Management Areas.
Nature Conservation Act 2002 (Tas)Protects biodiversity. It is an offence to introduce or spread declared weeds in reserved areas. Establishes the Tasmanian Weed Management Strategy.
Environmental Management and Pollution Control Act 1994 (Tas)Allows EPA to declare weed infestations an environmental nuisance and direct the occupier to control or eradicate the weed.
Biological Control Act 1984 (Cth)Regulates the introduction of biological control agents. Any new biocontrol agent requires approval.
Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (Cth)Allows the Commonwealth to identify and list invasive species and develop recovery plans. Protects threatened species and ecological communities, including habitat restoration (which may include weed removal).
General Biosecurity Duty (GBD)

Under the Biosecurity Act 2019, everyone has a legal obligation to take reasonable and practicable measures to prevent, eliminate, or minimise biosecurity risks. For declared weeds, this means you must control them on your land.

What "reasonable and practicable" means: The measures you take should be proportionate to the risk. For a small, isolated infestation of a highly invasive weed, eradication may be required. For a widespread infestation, containment and prevention of spread may be acceptable.

Critical: Failure to control declared weeds can result in enforcement action, including fines and government-directed control at the landholder's expense. Local councils can also issue Weed Control Notices.

Declared weeds in Tasmania include: Gorse, Montpelier Broom, Ragwort, Pampas Grass, Boneseed, Blackberry, Serrated Tussock, Heather, and others. For each species, the Statutory Weed Management Plan specifies whether your municipality is Zone A (eradication objective) or Zone B (containment objective). Check the NRE Tas Declared Weeds Index and the relevant SWMP for your area.

Record-keeping requirements

Commercial operators must maintain records of herbicide use including:

  • Date and location of application
  • Product name and rate
  • Weather conditions (wind speed/direction, temperature)
  • Equipment used
  • Operator name and qualifications

Records must be kept for at least 3 years. This is a legal requirement under the Agricultural and Veterinary Chemicals (Control of Use) Act 1995.

Codes of Practice

Code of Practice for Ground Spraying

Minimum standards for ground-based spraying in Tasmania. Key requirements:

  • Only use registered products for the specific weed and situation
  • Follow label instructions (rate, application method, buffer zones)
  • Maintain equipment in good condition
  • Prevent off-target movement (drift, runoff)
  • Do not spray waterways unless the product is registered for aquatic use
  • Keep records of all applications
Hazardous Manual Tasks Code of Practice

Applies to all weed control work involving manual handling. Requires:

  • Risk assessment of manual tasks (lifting, carrying, repetitive actions)
  • Control measures to prevent injury (mechanical aids, team lifting, correct technique)
  • Training and instruction for workers

Relevance to weed control: Hand pulling, carrying spray packs, moving equipment, and working on steep terrain all involve manual handling risks. These must be assessed and controlled.

Buffer zones and waterways

Herbicides can contaminate waterways and harm aquatic life. Buffer zones are specified on product labels and vary by product and situation.

Typical buffer zones: 5-100 metres from waterways, depending on the product, application method, and sensitivity of the waterway. Always read the label.

Tasmanian context: Never spray herbicides near waterways without checking the label's aquatic buffer zone. Some chemicals cannot be used within 20-100m of water in Tasmania. Civil penalties apply under the Water Management Act 1999 and Biosecurity Act 2019. Use only aquatic-registered herbicides if spraying near water.

Monitoring and evaluation

Monitoring is how you know if your control worked. It's not optional — it's central to IPM. Without monitoring, you're guessing.

Why monitor?
  • Assess effectiveness — Did the treatment achieve the desired kill rate?
  • Detect adverse impacts — Did you harm non-target species? Was there off-target drift?
  • Identify follow-up needs — Where is regrowth occurring? Where are new seedlings emerging?
  • Inform adaptive management — What should you do differently next time?
  • Meet legal requirements — Document your control efforts for compliance and reporting.
  • Build institutional knowledge — Records of what worked (and what didn't) are valuable for future programs.
How to monitor

Visual inspection: Walk the site systematically. Observe weed density, look for regrowth or new seedlings, check for damage to non-target plants.

Weed audit (quadrats): Count plants in defined areas (e.g., 1m² quadrats) to quantify density and track changes over time. Use a consistent method and record locations.

Photo points: Take photos from fixed locations (GPS-marked) at the same time each year. This provides a visual record of change.

Transects: Walk a fixed line across the site and record weeds encountered. Useful for large areas.

Remote sensing: Drones or satellite imagery for large-scale monitoring. Useful for detecting new infestations or tracking spread.

Monitoring schedule

Immediate (same day or next day): Check application — were all target plants treated? Was there any obvious off-target damage? Record weather conditions.

3 months: Assess kill rate — are treated plants dead or dying? Are there survivors? Look for regrowth. This is when you can see if the treatment worked.

6-12 months: Assess regrowth and seedbank germination. This is when new seedlings from the seedbank typically emerge. Plan follow-up treatment.

Annually (for multi-year programs): Assess overall progress toward objectives. Adjust strategy if needed. Document for reporting.

Pro tip: Set calendar reminders for monitoring. It's easy to forget once the initial treatment is done. Monitoring is part of the job, not an optional extra.

Case study: Monitoring a gorse control program

Site: 2ha gorse infestation, Derwent Valley, Tasmania.

Initial treatment (Year 1, autumn): Cut stump and basal bark on dense stands; foliar spray on isolated plants.

Monitoring results:

  • Immediate (Week 1): All visible plants treated. No off-target damage observed. Weather conditions: 18°C, light wind, no rain for 24 hours.
  • 3 months: 70% of treated plants dead or dying. Some regrowth from stumps that were not treated (missed). Plan follow-up.
  • 12 months: 85% kill overall. Dense seedling germination (expected from seedbank). Follow-up foliar spray on seedlings (spring).
  • Year 3: 95% reduction in adult plants. Seedlings continue to emerge but at lower density. Spot spray as needed.
  • Year 5: Occasional seedlings only. Monitor annually. Consider revegetation with native species to compete with remaining weeds.

Lessons: The missed stumps at 3 months show the importance of thorough application. The seedling flush at 12 months is normal and expected — it doesn't mean the treatment failed. Follow-up is essential.

Resources & glossary

Key terms
IPM
Integrated Pest Management — a strategic approach combining multiple control methods, monitoring, and thresholds.
Threshold
The level of infestation at which control action is justified. Zero for declared weeds; economic or ecological thresholds for others.
Seedbank
Viable weed seeds stored in the soil, capable of germinating years later. Gorse seed can persist 30-40+ years.
Non-target species
Plants or animals that are not the intended target of control. Native vegetation, threatened species, beneficial insects.
Buffer zone
A designated area (usually specified on the herbicide label) where chemical application is restricted to protect sensitive areas (waterways, native vegetation).
General Biosecurity Duty
Legal obligation under the Biosecurity Act 2019 to take reasonable and practicable measures to prevent, eliminate, or minimise biosecurity risks.
Declared weed
A weed listed under Tasmanian legislation, requiring control by law. Landholders must control declared weeds on their land.
Statutory Weed Management Plan (SWMP)
A legal document under the Biosecurity Act 2019 that specifies how a declared weed must be managed in each Tasmanian municipality. Zone A = eradication objective; Zone B = containment objective. Note: Older resources may refer to these as being under the repealed Weed Management Act 1999, but the plans themselves remain current law.
Biological control
Using natural enemies (insects, mites, diseases) to control weeds. Requires approval and is usually slow-acting.
Mode of Action (MOA)
The way a herbicide kills a plant. Rotating MOA groups reduces resistance risk.
Resistance
The ability of a weed population to survive a herbicide that would normally kill it. Develops through repeated selection pressure.
Cut stump / cut and paint
A targeted herbicide application method where the stem is cut and herbicide is applied immediately to the cut surface. Suitable for woody weeds.
Basal bark
A herbicide application method where the lower 30-40cm of the stem is sprayed with an oil-based herbicide mix. Suitable for stems <10cm diameter.

Case Study: Blackberries along a fenceline

Apply your learning to a real-world scenario from the assessment.

Scenario

It is August, and you are working for a landowner in Tasmania who wants to eradicate an infestation of blackberries along 300 metres of fence line. In places, the fence runs through the blackberries.

One side of the fence is pasture with cattle grazing. The other side is a steep sloping riverbank. The landowner has worked hard to restore riparian vegetation to improve river health. They have successfully planted several endemic species along the riverbank. Native bird and animal species are now regularly seen in the area.

The landowner has said they have a zero-tolerance threshold for the blackberries.

Non-target species

List five likely non-target species at this site:

  1. Native grasses (e.g., Poa species)
  2. Sedges and rushes
  3. Reeds
  4. Native shrubs (e.g., Leptospermum, Acacia, Melaleuca)
  5. Eucalypts (e.g., Blue Gum)

Why this matters: Any control method must minimise harm to these species. The riparian revegetation represents a significant investment of time and money — damaging it would be counterproductive.

Potential impacts

Current and potential impacts of the infestation:

  • Fire hazard — Dense dry biomass, particularly in summer
  • Competition with pasture — Reduced grazing area, lower carrying capacity
  • Habitat threat — Smothering native vegetation, reducing biodiversity
  • Environmental threat — Bank destabilization, erosion risk
  • Infrastructure damage — Deterioration of fence, restricting access

Case Study: Boneseed in a coastal reserve

A non-agricultural scenario focused on conservation outcomes in a Tasmanian coastal reserve.

Scenario

You are working as a conservation field officer for a local council in eastern Tasmania. A 12-hectare coastal reserve (open woodland over native grasses and sedges, with a narrow beach access track) has a large, established boneseed (Chrysanthemoides monilifera) infestation. Boneseed is a declared weed under the Tasmanian Biosecurity Act 2019.

The reserve is:

  • Adjacent to a public beach and walking track — recreational users, dogs, and school groups use it daily.
  • Home to a small population of a nationally listed threatened plant (a coastal Poa species), located along the upper edge of the beach access track.
  • Grazed historically — the local Aboriginal community has cultural connection to the coastal environment and uses the area for cultural practices. A cultural heritage assessment is on file but a walk-through is required before ground disturbance.
  • In a regional council weed management plan that lists boneseed as a containment target, with the objective of reducing the seedbank and preventing spread into adjoining conservation areas.

It is late winter (August). Some boneseed plants are flowering; most are in bud. The council has a budget for 30 person-days of field work plus herbicide and a small machinery hire allowance. The site has no water supply.

Task 1: Identify the site constraints

Before selecting a method, list the constraints that will shape your decision. Consider:

  • Public safety (recreation, school groups, dogs)
  • Heritage and cultural values
  • Threatened species protection
  • Council's containment objective under its weed management plan
  • No on-site water supply (affects herbicide mixing and equipment cleaning)
  • Coastal environment — wind, salt spray, sandy soils, and proximity to a marine environment
  • Legal obligations under the Biosecurity Act 2019 and Nature Conservation Act 2002
Hint: Boneseed is bird-dispersed via its black berries. Preventing seed set and berry production is the highest priority. This means control must happen before berries form.

Task 2: Compare potential control methods

Method Potential benefits Risks and constraints Legislation / WHS
Hand pulling / hand grubbing (seedlings and small plants) No chemical; suitable near threatened Poa; low off-target risk; minimal soil disturbance if done carefully Labour-intensive; only effective on small plants; roots may remain; may not be feasible for large dense stands; soil disturbance on dunes increases erosion risk Manual handling code; cultural heritage walk-through required
Cut stump / cut-and-paint (larger plants) Targeted; low off-target risk; suitable near threatened species and public areas; effective on woody stems Labour-intensive; requires immediate herbicide application to cut surface; thorn/stem handling injuries; chemical handling near the public AgVet Chemicals Act; label directions; PPE; record-keeping
Foliar spray (triclopyr or metsulfuron-based mix) Efficient for dense stands; can cover large areas; effective on actively growing plants Drift risk near the beach access track and public; risk to threatened Poa and non-target natives; marine environment sensitivity; no on-site water for mixing or rinsing; public exclusion required AgVet Chemicals Act; buffer zones (check label); council OHS procedures; public notification
Biological control Long-term; no chemical; potentially self-sustaining Not currently available for boneseed in Tasmania at a scale that would meet the containment objective; slow-acting; requires approval Biological Control Act 1984 (Cth)
Grazing Could reduce biomass Not appropriate in a public coastal reserve; risk to threatened Poa and public safety; animal welfare; likely to spread berries in dung Not permitted in the reserve
Mechanical (slashing / brushcutting) Fast biomass reduction Stimulates resprouting; spreads berries if seed heads present; creates bare ground; not recommended for boneseed in conservation areas unless followed by cut stump Manual handling; public safety; noise

Task 3: Recommendation plan

Prepare a recommendation plan that includes:

  • Target threshold — What is "success" under council's containment objective? (Hint: it will not be 0% eradication — it will be preventing spread and reducing the seedbank.)
  • Initial control methods — Which methods, in what order, and why?
  • Timing — Why August is critical given boneseed's flowering and berry cycle.
  • Monitoring — What will you look for, and when? Include both effectiveness and non-target impact.
  • Follow-up — Realistic schedule over the next 3–5 years.
  • Community and heritage — How will you notify recreational users, and what is your process for heritage walk-through?
  • Prevention — What will you do to prevent new boneseed establishing (especially via bird-dispersed berries from adjoining land)?

Task 4: Model answer (compare after you've drafted your own)

Target threshold: Containment — no seed set on site, no spread into adjoining conservation areas, measurable reduction in seedling density year-on-year over 5 years.

Initial methods:

  1. Hand grub seedlings and small plants in the threatened Poa zone and along the beach access track — no chemical, minimal disturbance, done first before larger plants are treated so seed isn't spread during later operations.
  2. Cut stump and cut-and-paint larger boneseed plants throughout the reserve — targeted, low off-target risk, suitable for the coastal environment and public areas.
  3. Foliar spray only in dense stands away from the public, the threatened Poa, and the marine environment; use a low-drift nozzle and carry water for rinsing; close the track or signpost during application.
  4. Bag and remove all flowering and fruiting material — off-site disposal to prevent berry spread.

Timing: August is late winter — boneseed is in bud/early flower, actively growing, and before berry formation. This is the ideal window: systemic herbicides will translocate, and berry spread is prevented. If August work is delayed, prioritise flower/berry head removal first.

Monitoring:

  • Immediately after treatment — visual check for missed plants, off-target damage, berry spill.
  • 3 months — assess kill rate and resprouting; check threatened Poa for damage.
  • 12 months — seedling count in fixed 1m² quadrats; berry check on any surviving plants; report to council.
  • Annually for 5 years — trend assessment against containment objective.

Follow-up: Hand pull and spot spray regrowth at 6 and 12 months, then annually. Re-treat any new boneseed seedlings from bird-dispersed seed along the reserve edges and beach access track. Keep the zone adjacent to the threatened Poa chemical-free.

Community and heritage: Post signage at reserve entrances and the beach access track at least 48 hours before herbicide work; brief school groups and dog walkers. Complete a heritage walk-through with the local Aboriginal community representative before any ground disturbance. Record the walk-through in the project file.

Prevention: Annual edge walk of the reserve to catch bird-dispersed seedlings early. Engage adjoining land managers to control their own boneseed. Maintain ground cover in treated areas to reduce bare ground and erosion. Include boneseed in the council's annual weed surveillance schedule.

Key contrast with the agricultural case study: The objective here is containment, not eradication; public safety and heritage values are central constraints; the threatened Poa dictates chemical-free zones; and bird dispersal creates ongoing re-invasion pressure that must be managed indefinitely. This is typical of non-agricultural weed programs in Tasmanian reserves.

Reflection questions

  • How would your plan differ if council's objective changed from containment to eradication?
  • What would you do if a resident reported that school children had eaten berries from a treated plant?
  • How would you adjust your method choice if the reserve were in a high-wind coastal location versus an inland sheltered site?
  • Why is it important that heritage walk-through happens before rather than after ground disturbance?
  • What are the risks of doing foliar spray first and cut stump second, instead of the reverse?

Takeaway: "Eradication" of gorse is a phrase that should make you nervous. The realistic objective is a long-term program that keeps the shallow layer exhausted while systematically reducing the deep layer — for a decade or more. The 3D model shows you why.

Why it matters

Once the cut surface oxidises and sap seals the wound, the herbicide sits on top and doesn't move into the root system. You get a dead top and a re-shooting stump — the worst of both worlds. This is why contractors carry the paste with the saw, not in the truck.

Try this: cut the stump and wait. Watch the timer go from green, to amber, to red. When you paint at red, efficacy is well below 50% — the cut surface is effectively closed. That's exactly what happens in the field, and it's invisible until the stump resprouts months later.
